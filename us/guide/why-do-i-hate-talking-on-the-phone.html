Why do I hate talking on the phone, and how do I get better at it?

T.Beet EditorsNov 26, 2026 · 3 min read

30-SECOND SUMMARY

Most people who hate the phone are really afraid of being judged in real time, with no face to read and no time to edit. The fix is mostly practice: short, low-stakes calls, one small habit at a time.

01Why does the phone feel so much harder than texting?

Texting gives you time. You can reread, delete, and try again. A call gives you none of that, so every pause feels loud.

You also lose the face. In person, a smile tells you things are going fine. On the phone, silence could mean anything, and an anxious brain fills the gap with the worst guess.

Here's my honest take: the dislike is rarely about the phone itself. It's about the feeling of being watched while you think. Once you see that, it gets easier to work on.

02Why does avoiding calls make it worse?

Every time you skip a call, you feel relief. Your brain learns that calls were dangerous and skipping was the smart move. Next time, the dread is a little louder.

That's why "I'll just text instead" works as a short-term fix and a long-term trap. We're not saying you must love calls. We're saying a few good ones can quiet the alarm.

03How do you get more comfortable, step by step?

Don't start with the scariest call. Build up, the way you would with any other skill.

StepWhat to try
1Send a voice memo to a friend instead of a text.
2Make a short call to someone you trust, with a clear reason to hang up.
3Get specific about what bugs you: silence, interrupting, your own voice?
4Write one opening line and one follow-up question. Not a script, just a way in.
5Try a call with someone new, and keep it short on purpose.

Step 3 matters more than it looks. "I hate calls" is too big to fix. "I hate awkward silence" is something you can work on, and the answer is often a simple follow-up question.

Two of our other guides can help here: how to stop being nervous before a first call and easy first-call openers.

04What if the dread is really strong?

If the thought of a call affects your work or your daily life, it's worth talking to a therapist. Phone anxiety is well known, and there are proven ways to treat it. Needing help is not a flaw.

For everyone else, remember this: you don't have to sound smooth. Being warm, curious, and a little imperfect is more than enough. Most people on the other end are listening for kindness, not polish.

05Where does a short voice-only call fit in?

A call with a clear end is easier to face. You know you only have to hold on for a few minutes, and you can't be judged on your outfit or your photos.

It's not therapy, and it won't promise you a match. It's just a gentle place to practice talking to someone new.

Q&AFAQ

Is it normal to hate talking on the phone?

Yes. Plenty of people find calls more stressful than texting, because you have to answer in real time with no face to read. It's common, and it can get easier with practice.

Should I just avoid calls and stick to texting?

Texting is fine for plans and quick check-ins. But avoiding calls entirely tends to make the dread bigger, so a few short, low-stakes calls are a kind way to practice.

Can a 3-minute call help with phone anxiety?

A short, voice-only call can feel more manageable than a long one, because the end is already in sight. On Beet, a 1:1 call ends at 3 minutes, and you don't have to say yes to anything afterward.
